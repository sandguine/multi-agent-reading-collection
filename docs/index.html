Exercise · 10 minutes

Commitment in Chicken

Two players choose Swerve or Straight. Payoffs are (2, 2) for two swerves, (1, 3) for row swerving against column going straight, (3, 1) for the reverse, and (0, 0) for two straight choices.

  1. Find the pure-strategy Nash equilibria.
  2. Suppose row publicly and irrevocably commits to Straight before column chooses. What is column’s best response?
  3. Explain why two independently binding commitments can be worse for both players.
Show solution / discussion

The pure equilibria are (Straight, Swerve) and (Swerve, Straight): against Swerve, Straight yields 3 rather than 2; against Straight, Swerve yields 1 rather than 0. After row’s binding commitment, column chooses Swerve, yielding (3, 1). If both commit to Straight before responding to the other, the result is (0, 0). The leader advantage relies on observability, irreversibility, and the timing of the follower’s choice. A retractable announcement does not change the feasible action set in the same way.

Exercise · 10 minutes

What a pair approximation assumes

For three binary variables X, Y, Z, a pair closure uses

P(X, Y, Z) ≈ P(X, Y) P(Y, Z) / P(Y).

  1. What conditional independence would make this exact when P(Y) > 0?
  2. Let X be a fair bit, set Z = X, and let Y be an independent fair bit. Compare the true P(0, 0, 0) with the pair approximation.
Show solution / discussion

The expression is exact if X and Z are conditionally independent given Y. In the example, P(X = 0, Y = 0) = P(Y = 0, Z = 0) = 1/4 and P(Y = 0) = 1/2, so the closure gives 1/8. The true probability is 1/4 because Z is fixed by X. These two adjacent pair marginals fail to encode the remaining dependence. A successful approximation therefore needs evidence about the dependencies it discards.

Companion exercise for Chu et al.; illustrative probability model.

Exercise · 10 minutes

Average over the fast process

For fixed learning variables, a two-state Markov chain has transition matrix P = [[0.9, 0.1], [0.3, 0.7]]. The per-step rewards in states 0 and 1 are 0 and 4.

  1. Find the stationary distribution and its average reward.
  2. Explain what must be checked before replacing state-dependent updates by this average while Q-values change.
Show solution / discussion

Stationarity requires 0.1π₀ = 0.3π₁ and π₀ + π₁ = 1, giving π = (3/4, 1/4). The average reward is 1. Averaging while Q changes requires the state process to approach the relevant stationary distribution sufficiently quickly relative to changes in Q. If transitions depend on Q, the stationary distribution must be recomputed at the current slow variables. Non-mixing regions, long transients, or rapid policy changes can undermine this approximation.

Exercise · 10–15 minutes

From a test-set score to deployment risk

A monitor detects 99% of attacks in a fixed test set. A proposed deployment lets an untrusted actor choose its attack timing and inputs, and another instance of the same model performs monitoring.

  1. Give two reasons why the score alone does not bound the probability of at least one successful deployment attack.
  2. If each of N opportunities has failure probability at most ε, what general upper bound can you give without assuming independence?
  3. What would an evaluation need to justify about those per-opportunity probabilities?
Show solution / discussion

The attacker may select inputs unlike the test set or exploit shared recognition and collusion opportunities; a long deployment also creates many opportunities. The union bound gives P(at least one failure) ≤ min(1, Nε), with no independence assumption, provided the per-event marginal bounds apply in the actual deployment. For adaptive interaction, a uniform conditional bound over relevant histories is one sufficient route to those marginal bounds. Test-set accuracy alone does not establish that premise, the opportunity count, or coverage of the attacker’s strategies.

Exercise · 15–20 minutes

Payoff error and the missing-policy problem

For a finite restricted game, assume |ûᵢ(a) − uᵢ(a)| ≤ η for every player i and joint pure strategy a. For a mixed profile σ, define maximum unilateral regret as

r(σ) = maxᵢ maxₐᵢ [uᵢ(aᵢ, σ₋ᵢ) − uᵢ(σ)].

  1. Show that |r̂(σ) − r(σ)| ≤ 2η.
  2. What follows when r̂(σ) = 0?
  3. Construct a reason that this does not certify deviations to policies absent from the restricted game.
Show solution / discussion

Taking expectations preserves the uniform η error bound. Each deviation gain subtracts two expected payoffs, so its error is at most 2η by the triangle inequality. For corresponding finite sets of numbers, the difference of their maxima is bounded by their largest pointwise difference; apply this over players and deviations. Thus |r̂ − r| ≤ 2η and an exact empirical equilibrium has true restricted-game regret at most 2η. An omitted policy can yield arbitrarily more than any included policy without violating a bound that only covers included profiles. Sampling reduces estimation uncertainty; policy exploration addresses a different gap.

Exercise · 10–15 minutes

A belief state is not a point estimate

An agent is uncertain about a fixed binary state S, with P(S = 1) = 0.6. It can guess the state immediately for reward 1 if correct and 0 otherwise, or buy a perfect observation at cost 0.1 and then guess.

  1. Compare the values of guessing immediately and observing first.
  2. What would be missed by simply treating the most probable state as known?
  3. Does the existence of an optimal belief-based policy establish that a neural agent contains an explicit Bayesian filter?
Show solution / discussion

Guessing 1 immediately has expected reward 0.6. Observing then guessing has expected net reward 1 − 0.1 = 0.9. Treating the modal state as certain would erase the value of information. General partially observed planning must account for how actions affect future information, even when a belief is a sufficient state for planning. The existence of a belief-based representation does not identify a particular implementation; evidence about computation is still required.

Exercise · 15–20 minutes

Same choice, different mechanism

An agent can help another at a personal cost of 2, producing a benefit of 5 for the recipient. Model A values own payoff plus α times the recipient’s payoff. Model B values only own payoff but expects a penalty F if it refuses to help.

  1. For which α and F does helping weakly maximize utility?
  2. Why does observing help once fail to distinguish the models?
  3. Propose an intervention and state the assumptions needed to interpret it.
Show solution / discussion

Relative to refusing, Model A gains −2 + 5α and helps for α ≥ 2/5. Model B gains F − 2 and helps for F ≥ 2. Many parameter choices therefore predict the same observed action. Remove the expected penalty while holding costs, recipient benefit, and the agent’s other beliefs fixed: Model B then refuses, while Model A with α > 2/5 still helps. Interpretation requires the agent to believe the penalty is gone, preferences to remain stable, and the manipulation not to change other incentives. A failed manipulation or a third mechanism can preserve observational equivalence.

Exercise · 10–15 minutes

A collective explanation that could fail

A group repeatedly coordinates on a shared plan. One explanation treats it as a collective agent; another attributes the behavior to a common prompt and locally imitative policies.

Specify a high-level prediction and an intervention that could make the two accounts disagree. State what would count against your proposed collective model.

Show solution / discussion

For example, define a collective model that predicts flexible pursuit of a specified group objective when responsibilities are reassigned. Intervene on who has access to an opportunity or resource, while testing whether the group reorganizes to preserve that objective. Compare with an explicit imitation model under the same intervention. A failure of the collective model’s predicted adaptation counts against that model, provided the intervention remains in its stated scope. A success does not uniquely establish collective agency: local mechanisms may reproduce it, and alternative models must also be tested.
